FIELD NOTES · NO. 04

Anatomy of a Tree

A cross-section guide to what's underneath the bark.

The Trunk The Leaf The Roots Reading the Rings

Cut any trunk straight across and you'll find the tree keeping a written record of itself. Every ring is a year; every layer has a job. The outer bark is armor. Just beneath it, a thin green cambium divides itself into new wood on one side and new bark on the other, the only part of the trunk that is technically alive.

Sapwood carries water upward from root to leaf, season after season, until it's eventually retired inward and sealed off. What's left behind at the center no longer moves anything at all. It has gone quiet, dense, and dark — a structural memory the tree keeps, not for growing, but simply for standing.

Specimen: Quercus robur, English oak. Cross-section taken at breast height, sixty-one growth rings visible.

The Leaf: Photosynthesis's Factory Floor

Slice a leaf the way you'd slice the trunk and the same principle holds: every layer has a job. A waxy cuticle on top cuts down on water loss. Beneath it, a tight row of upright palisade cells does most of the actual light-catching, packed with chloroplasts and stacked like books on a shelf to intercept as much sun as possible.

Lower down, the spongy mesophyll is looser and full of air pockets, built for gas exchange rather than light capture. Carbon dioxide diffuses in and oxygen diffuses out through pores called stomata, mostly on the underside of the leaf, each one flanked by a pair of guard cells that open and close it in response to light, humidity, and the tree's own water budget.

A single mature oak can carry 200,000 leaves and move several hundred litres of water a day, most of it lost right back out through these same stomata.

The Roots: A Mirror Underground

Roughly speaking, a tree's root system spreads out roughly as wide as its crown does, sometimes wider. A young taproot drives straight down for anchorage and access to deep water, then gives way over the years to a shallower, wide-reaching network of lateral roots — most of a mature tree's absorbing roots live in the top 30-60 cm of soil, right where water, oxygen, and nutrients are easiest to reach.

At the very tips of the finest roots, root hairs — single-cell extensions barely visible without a lens — do the actual work of pulling in water and dissolved minerals. Many species also lean on mycorrhizal fungi threaded through the soil around those tips, trading root-made sugars for a vastly larger effective surface area to draw from.

Reading the Rings: An Introduction to Dendrochronology

Every ring you counted in the cross-section above records one growing season: a pale band of large, fast-grown earlywood laid down in spring, followed by a darker, denser band of latewood as growth slows toward autumn. Line the two up and you get one visible ring per year — which is what makes a trunk countable in the first place.

Ring width carries information too. Wide rings usually mean a good year — plenty of rain, a long growing season, no competition crowding out the light. Narrow rings cluster around drought years, hard frosts, insect outbreaks, or the years right after a neighboring tree fell and this one had to redirect its energy toward the wound before it could put on new wood.

Dendrochronologists cross-match these wide/narrow patterns between living trees and old timber to build ring-width chronologies stretching back thousands of years.
